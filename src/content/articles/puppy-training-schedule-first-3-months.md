The first three months at home set the foundation for everything that follows. You don't need a rigid curriculum — but having a rough week-by-week structure prevents the common trap of training nothing consistently.

## Weeks 1–2: Settling In

Focus on the basics: name recognition, a consistent potty schedule, and building a positive association with the crate. Keep training sessions to 2–3 minutes, several times a day. This is also decompression time — don't overload a brand-new puppy with too much stimulation.

## Weeks 3–4: Foundational Cues

Introduce "sit" and hand-targeting using food lures, and start short, low-distraction leash exposure indoors. Continue reinforcing the potty schedule consistently — this is the period where habits either form well or start slipping.

## Weeks 5–6: Building Structure

Add "come" in short, high-value indoor sessions, and begin structured, supervised socialization (safe puppy classes, calm adult dogs, varied but manageable environments). Start introducing "leave it" with low-value items.

## Weeks 7–8: Expanding the World

Increase exposure to new sounds, surfaces, and people in short, positive doses. Continue basic obedience (sit, come, leave it) in slightly more distracting settings — a quiet backyard instead of just indoors.

## Weeks 9–12: Generalizing and Extending

Start practicing known cues in new locations to build generalization, introduce "stay" with very short durations, and begin structured leash walking practice outside the home. Crate tolerance should be extending toward a few calm hours by this stage.

## Common Mistakes Across This Period

- Trying to teach five new cues at once instead of building one solid skill before adding the next.
- Skipping socialization because of vaccination timing — talk to your vet about safe, controlled exposure during this critical window rather than avoiding it entirely.
- Inconsistent potty schedules on busy days, which slows progress more than almost anything else.

## Staying on Track

A loose weekly structure like this is meant as a guide, not a rigid deadline — every puppy progresses at their own pace. If you want a more detailed, structured curriculum to follow rather than assembling your own week by week, a dedicated puppy training program can fill in the specifics.
