Heeling is a more precise skill than general loose-leash walking — it means your dog stays in a specific position (typically at your side) and stays engaged with you, not just avoiding pulling. It's useful for controlled situations: crowded sidewalks, vet visits, or competition obedience.

## Heel vs. Loose Leash Walking

A loose leash walk just means no tension on the leash — your dog can sniff, wander within leash length, and explore. A heel means a specific position and sustained attention on you. Most dogs don't need to heel for an entire walk; it's a skill to call on for specific moments.

## Step-by-Step Method

1. **Start stationary.** With your dog on leash at your side, mark and reward for simply standing in heel position next to you, building the association before adding movement.
2. **Add single steps.** Take one step, and if your dog stays in position, mark and reward. Gradually increase the number of steps before rewarding.
3. **Use your body and treats to guide position.** Holding a treat at your side as a brief lure can help establish the position, fading the lure into a hand signal and eventually just the verbal cue.
4. **Reinforce eye contact and engagement**, not just physical position — a dog that's checking in with you is easier to keep in heel through distractions.
5. **Practice turns and stops.** Heeling in a straight line is easier than heeling through turns — practice direction changes once straight-line heeling is solid, since real-world walking rarely stays in a straight line.

## Common Mistakes

- Expecting heel position for an entire walk, which is unnecessary and can make walks feel like constant work for both of you.
- Jerking the leash to correct position instead of rewarding the correct position proactively.
- Skipping stationary and single-step practice, jumping straight to full walks before the basic mechanics are solid.
