"Leave it" and "drop it" solve two different problems — preventing your dog from taking something in the first place, versus getting them to release something they already have — and both are essential safety cues, especially around anything potentially dangerous.

## Teaching "Leave It"

1. **Start with a closed fist.** Hold a treat in your closed hand and let your dog sniff and lick at it. The moment they pull back or stop trying, mark ("yes!") and reward from your other hand — never the fist hand, so "leave it" doesn't mean "eventually you get this one."
2. **Add the cue word.** Once your dog reliably backs off from the closed fist, start saying "leave it" just before presenting it.
3. **Progress to a visible, low-value item on the floor.** Cover it with your foot if needed at first, saying "leave it," and reward for backing away.
4. **Increase difficulty gradually.** Move to higher-value items, then items dropped without covering, always rewarding generously for compliance.

## Teaching "Drop It"

1. **Start with a low-value toy** your dog is mildly interested in.
2. **Offer a trade.** Present a higher-value treat near your dog's nose while saying "drop it" — most dogs will release the toy to get the treat.
3. **Mark and reward the release**, then give the toy back sometimes, so "drop it" doesn't always mean losing the item permanently.
4. **Practice with a variety of items**, gradually including higher-value toys, so the cue generalizes beyond one specific object.

## Common Mistakes

- Prying an item out of your dog's mouth by force, which can create guarding behavior over time. See our guide on [resource guarding](../resource-guarding-in-dogs/) if this is already an issue.
- Only practicing "leave it" with food, then being surprised it doesn't transfer to picking up something on a walk.
- Never giving anything back after "drop it," which makes dogs less willing to release items voluntarily over time.

## Why These Cues Matter

Beyond convenience, "leave it" and "drop it" can prevent your dog from ingesting something dangerous — spoiled food, a dropped medication, or wildlife. If you want these safety cues built into a broader, structured obedience curriculum rather than taught in isolation, a step-by-step training program can help tie everything together.
