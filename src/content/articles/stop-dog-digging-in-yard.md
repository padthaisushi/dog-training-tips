Digging is rarely random — it's usually driven by boredom, instinct, temperature regulation, or trying to reach something interesting (a scent, a critter, cooler soil). Fixing it means addressing the underlying reason, not just filling in holes.

## Identify the Likely Cause

- **Boredom or excess energy:** digging spikes when exercise and mental stimulation are low.
- **Temperature:** many dogs dig shallow pits to lie in cool dirt during hot weather.
- **Prey drive:** digging concentrated at one spot (fence line, garden bed) often means your dog is after rodents or scent.
- **Anxiety:** digging near exits or fences can overlap with separation anxiety or escape attempts.

## Step-by-Step Plan

1. **Increase physical and mental exercise.** A tired, mentally satisfied dog digs far less. Add a daily walk, fetch session, or puzzle feeder if digging correlates with idle time.
2. **Create a legal digging spot.** Section off a sandbox or loose-soil area, bury toys or treats there, and redirect your dog to that spot whenever you catch them starting elsewhere.
3. **Address the trigger directly.** If it's heat, provide shade and cool mats. If it's rodents, consider humane pest deterrents. If it's escape-related, treat it as a separation or containment issue, not just digging.
4. **Supervise and interrupt early.** Catch your dog in the act, calmly redirect to the legal digging zone, and reward them for digging there instead.
5. **Avoid punishment after the fact.** Scolding a dog for a hole dug an hour ago teaches nothing except to be wary of you — timing has to be immediate to mean anything, and even then, redirection works better than punishment.

## Common Mistakes

- Filling holes without addressing why the dog is digging, leading to repeat holes in new spots.
- Leaving a bored dog outside unsupervised for long stretches, which is prime digging time.
- Using harsh corrections that increase anxiety-driven digging instead of reducing it.

## When It's More Than Digging

If digging is paired with other signs of distress — pacing, escape attempts, destructive behavior only when alone — it may be rooted in anxiety rather than boredom. A structured training program can help you sort out the underlying cause and build a targeted plan.
