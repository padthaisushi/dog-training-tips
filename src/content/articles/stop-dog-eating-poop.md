Coprophagia (eating feces) is unpleasant but common, and in most cases isn't a sign of a "bad" dog — it can stem from instinct, nutrition, learned habit, or occasionally an underlying medical issue. A combination of management and training is usually the most effective path.

## Rule Out Medical Causes First

Nutrient deficiencies, malabsorption issues, or parasites can contribute to coprophagia in some dogs. If the behavior is new, sudden, or paired with other symptoms (weight loss, appetite changes, diarrhea), a veterinary check is the right first step before assuming it's purely behavioral.

## Step-by-Step Management and Training Plan

1. **Clean up promptly.** The single most effective step is removing the opportunity — pick up waste in the yard immediately, and don't leave litter boxes (for multi-pet households) accessible.
2. **Strengthen "leave it" and "drop it."** A reliable "leave it" gives you a tool to redirect before your dog engages, and "drop it" helps if it's already too late — see our guides on [teaching leave it and drop it](../teach-leave-it-and-drop-it/) for the full method.
3. **Supervise outdoor time closely**, especially during the training period, so you can intervene before the behavior is rehearsed and reinforced.
4. **Increase overall enrichment.** Some cases are linked to boredom or attention-seeking — more structured exercise and mental stimulation can reduce the drive.
5. **Avoid punishment after the fact.** Since this typically happens quickly and discreetly, delayed punishment doesn't connect to the behavior and can add anxiety without solving the underlying issue.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming it's purely a "gross habit" without ruling out nutritional or medical contributors.
- Relying only on prevention (cleanup) without building the leave it/drop it skills that help in unsupervised moments.
- Punishing after discovering evidence of the behavior, which teaches nothing about the actual moment it happened.

## When to Get Help

If the behavior persists despite consistent management and training, or you're unsure whether a medical cause is involved, loop in your veterinarian. For the training side, a structured program that builds strong impulse-control cues can make ongoing management significantly easier.
