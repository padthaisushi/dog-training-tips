Multi-dog households run more smoothly with clear, consistent structure — most conflict comes from ambiguity around resources, attention, and space, not simply "too many dogs." A few deliberate household rules go a long way.

## Core Household Rules

1. **Feed separately.** Separate bowls, separate spaces (or supervised distance) at mealtimes removes the single most common trigger for tension in multi-dog homes.
2. **Give everyone their own resting space.** Multiple beds in different locations mean no dog has to compete for — or guard — the only comfortable spot.
3. **Train each dog individually, not just as a group.** Individual training sessions build stronger cue reliability than only ever practicing with the whole pack present.
4. **Manage high-value items proactively.** Chews, new toys, and food-stuffed puzzles are common flashpoints — supervise or separate dogs during these until you're confident there's no tension.
5. **Reinforce calm coexistence, not just the absence of conflict.** Reward dogs for resting calmly near each other, not only intervening when something goes wrong.

## Handling Attention and Affection

Avoid ignoring one dog to focus entirely on another, which can build frustration over time. Practice giving individual attention to each dog regularly, and teach a "wait your turn" pattern (like a sit-stay) for things like greetings or treats, so structure — not competition — determines who goes first.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming dogs will "work it out" without any household structure, which works for some pairs but creates ongoing tension in others.
- Only intervening after conflict starts, rather than managing the predictable triggers (food, space, high-value items) proactively.
- Inconsistent rules between family members, which undermines the structure dogs rely on to feel secure.

## When Tension Escalates

Occasional grumbling between housemates can be normal dog communication, but repeated fights, injury, or one dog consistently avoiding another are signs to intervene more seriously. A structured, professional training program — or a certified behaviorist for repeated fighting — can help identify the specific triggers and build a targeted management plan.
