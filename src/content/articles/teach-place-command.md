"Place" (or "go to your bed") is one of the highest-value cues you can teach — it gives your dog a clear job during mealtimes, guests arriving, or any moment you need them settled and out from underfoot.

## Step-by-Step Method

1. **Choose a consistent spot.** A dog bed, mat, or raised cot works well — consistency in the physical object helps your dog understand the cue quickly.
2. **Lure onto the spot.** Toss a treat onto the mat, and as your dog steps on and eats it, mark and reward again for staying there.
3. **Add the cue word.** Once your dog readily moves onto the mat for a tossed treat, start saying "place" just before tossing it, building the verbal association.
4. **Reward duration on the mat.** Gradually increase how long your dog stays there before releasing, rewarding intermittently for calm behavior while in position.
5. **Build real-world use gradually.** Practice "place" during low-key moments first (you're just sitting nearby), then build up to higher-distraction situations like meals or the doorbell ringing.

## Common Mistakes

- Only practicing in calm, empty rooms and expecting instant reliability with guests at the door.
- Releasing your dog the moment they whine or leave the mat on their own, which teaches that leaving early works.
- Skipping duration-building, so "place" only means "step on the mat briefly" rather than "stay here until released."

## Why This Cue Is So Useful

"Place" solves a huge range of everyday problems — door greetings, begging at the table, general underfoot chaos — by giving your dog a clear, well-reinforced alternative to whatever they'd otherwise be doing. It pairs especially well with our guide on [stopping jumping on people](../stop-dog-jumping-on-people/), since a solid place cue gives your dog somewhere to be instead of at the door.

## Building It Into a Full Routine

Once trained, "place" becomes a daily-use tool rather than just a trick — many owners use it multiple times a day. If you want it built into a complete household-manners curriculum, a structured training program can help sequence it alongside your other cues.
