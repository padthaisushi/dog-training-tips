Resource guarding — stiffening, growling, snapping, or lunging when someone approaches food, toys, or a resting spot — is a natural instinct taken to an uncomfortable extreme. It's manageable in most cases, but it needs a careful, structured approach rather than confrontation.

## Understand What's Happening

Resource guarding is rooted in anxiety about losing something valuable, not dominance. Punishing a dog for guarding (yelling, forcibly taking the item) usually escalates the behavior, because it confirms the dog's fear that people approaching means losing the resource.

## Step-by-Step Plan

1. **Stop testing or provoking it.** Avoid the common but counterproductive advice to "take the bowl away to show them who's boss" — this makes guarding worse, not better.
2. **Build a "approach means more" association.** While your dog eats, calmly walk by and drop an extra high-value treat into the bowl without reaching toward it, then walk away. Repeat over many meals.
3. **Practice a trade-up cue.** Teach "drop it" by offering a higher-value item in exchange for what your dog has, rather than taking things away empty-handed.
4. **Manage the environment during training.** Feed in a low-traffic area, and don't let young children approach a dog that's eating or has a valued item during this training period.
5. **Generalize gradually.** Once your dog is relaxed with people near their food bowl, apply the same approach-and-add technique to toys and resting spots, one resource at a time.

## Common Mistakes

- Confronting or physically correcting a guarding dog, which increases the risk of a bite.
- Only practicing with adults, then being surprised when guarding shows up around children or strangers.
- Stopping training as soon as the obvious signs (growling) disappear, without confirming the underlying tension is actually resolved.

## When to Get Professional Help

Resource guarding that includes biting, or that's directed at children, is a safety issue — not just a training inconvenience. In those cases, working through a structured, professional program (or consulting a certified behaviorist) is strongly recommended rather than troubleshooting alone.
