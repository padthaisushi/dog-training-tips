Guarding a couch or bed is a form of resource guarding focused on space rather than food or toys — and it responds to the same underlying principle: reduce the anxiety about losing the spot, rather than confronting the dog directly.

## Understand the Root Cause

Space guarding usually reflects anxiety about being displaced from a comfortable, valued spot — not a bid for "dominance." Confrontational responses (forcibly pulling a dog off furniture, aggressive corrections) tend to escalate the behavior because they confirm the dog's fear that someone approaching means losing the spot.

## Step-by-Step Plan

1. **Stop testing or provoking it.** Avoid deliberately trying to push your dog off furniture to "prove a point" — this reinforces the underlying fear driving the behavior.
2. **Teach a reliable "off" cue away from the guarding context first.** Practice "off" from low-value surfaces with rewards for compliance before applying it where guarding happens.
3. **Trade up instead of confronting.** Call your dog off with a high-value reward or invite them to a different comfortable spot, rather than physically removing them.
4. **Build a positive association with people approaching the furniture.** While your dog is calmly resting there, toss a treat toward them from a distance without reaching in — repeat over many sessions to build trust.
5. **Set clear furniture rules consistently.** If the goal is no furniture access at all, apply that consistently from the start using management (baby gates, an appealing dog bed) rather than repeated confrontation over an allowed-then-revoked privilege.

## Common Mistakes

- Physically forcing a dog off furniture, which risks a bite and reinforces the guarding association.
- Inconsistent household rules — some family members allowing furniture access, others not — which creates confusion and can heighten anxiety.
- Only addressing the visible behavior (growling) without working on the underlying trust and predictability.

## When to Get Professional Help

If guarding includes biting or snapping, or is directed at children, treat it as a safety issue rather than a simple training inconvenience. In these cases, a structured, professional training program — or a certified behaviorist consult — is the safer path forward.
